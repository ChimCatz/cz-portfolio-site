Advanced Excel / CRM Workflow

Turning Excel Into a CRM

Case Study Download Sample Workbook (Lite Version)

Before LeadFlow ETL existed, someone still had to compile and maintain the CRM data it would eventually automate. That someone was me, and the tool was Excel. Every month, lead records came in from six different sources, Lusha, Pipileads, Vtiger, Zoho, Datamine, and purchased lists, each with its own format, its own quirks, and no shared structure. The compilation workbook grew to a database of 30,000+ records across 160+ tracked fields, and it had to stay accurate, deduplicated, and reportable without any external tooling. This is that system.

The core challenge was consistency. The same company would show up under slightly different names depending on the source, "Redwood Group Pte Ltd" from one export, "Redwood Group Pte. Ltd." from another. Manually normalizing that at scale was not realistic, so I built a cleanup formula that strips common legal suffixes automatically. It combines TRIM, LEFT, and an array-entered FIND against a maintained keyword list of common legal suffixes (Pte, Ltd, Sdn Bhd, Inc, Limited, Tbk, PLC, and more), checking a company name against every suffix in one pass and trimming it at the earliest match. Adding a new suffix pattern later just meant adding a row to the keyword list, not rewriting the formula.

Reporting was the second problem. Leadership needed to see how many active and inactive leads existed per source, per year, without opening the raw sheet. I built a dashboard sheet that uses SUMIFS across the full database, cross-referencing a computed year field against each source and status combination. Every cell in that dashboard recalculates live as new records get added or a status flips from Active to Inactive, so the summary view never goes stale relative to the raw data.

One real quirk this shape reflects: the raw source field stores "Vtiger" and "vtiger" as two different-cased values in places, a manual-entry inconsistency the SUMIFS dashboard and lookup formulas have to tolerate rather than assume away.

Day-to-day, the workbook was paired with a task-tracking sheet that logged daily record counts by source and status, covering routines like database allocation, updating inactive contacts from Datamine, and tracking leads through Vtiger and Zoho separately, so drift or stalled updates were visible immediately instead of surfacing weeks later during a reporting cycle. That operational habit, tracking the database like a living system instead of a static file, is what eventually made the case for automating the whole pipeline in the first place. The patterns I leaned on most in Excel, source-aware cleanup rules, a normalized identity field, and status-based aggregation, are the same ones LeadFlow ETL later reimplemented in Python at a much larger scale.
